Methods

How the Sierra Mosquito Nowcast severity index is computed, and how it's been checked.

Model

Severity is computed with a transparent, rule-based formula, not a fitted statistical or machine-learning model: 

mosquito_potential = emergence_score(days_since_snowmelt, degree_days_since_melt)
                    * habitat_multiplier
                    * moisture_multiplier
                    * current_activity_multiplier

severity_score = mosquito_potential * 5.0   (clamped to 0-5)
ComponentSourceWhat it captures
Snowmelt dateMODIS MOD10A2, 8-day snow cover Melt-out = the day after the last day snow was observed this season (scanning backward from today, not the first snow-free stretch forward -- Sierra snowpack routinely has brief early-season thaws before more snow falls)
Degree-days since meltgridMET daily temperature Heat accumulation since melt-out; the primary driver of the emergence curve
HabitatNLCD land cover, JRC Global Surface Water, NASADEM slope Wet meadow/marsh, lake edge, forested, steep/rocky, or open-dry classification
MoisturegridMET precipitation Recent rain / dry-spell adjustment
Current activitygridMET wind & temperature Wind in particular reduces experienced biting activity independent of abundance

Validation data: High Sierra Topix

High Sierra Topix runs an annual "Mosquito Reports" thread where hikers self-report conditions on a standardized 0–5 scale. We extracted 548 place/date/severity observations from 147 of 151 individual trip reports across the 2024 and 2025 threads, geocoded the place names against the USGS GNIS gazetteer (fuzzy-matched, confidence-banded -- vague route descriptions were left ungeocoded rather than forced to a point), and scored each one with the model as of its actual report date, using only data available up to that date (no lookahead).

This dataset was used only to validate the model, not to train it. No regression or fitting procedure was run against it to produce the deployed parameters. The emergence curve, habitat multipliers, and thresholds in the deployed model are the same literature/hypothesis-driven values described in the project plan, not values fit to these 548 reports.

What the validation found

Spearman rank correlation between predicted and reported severity: ~0.23 (p < 0.001) — a real, statistically significant relationship, but a weak one. Three-bucket (low/moderate/high) agreement: ~43%.

Two concrete things came out of this exercise:

  • A real bug, fixed: the habitat classifier treated any point within 500m of open water as high-multiplier "lake edge / slow water" habitat -- but most named places in trip reports resolve to the lake itself, where "water within 500m" is trivially true. Steep, rocky-shored alpine lakes (poor mosquito habitat) were getting the same bonus as genuine marshy lake edges. Fixed by adding a slope-based lake_rocky_shore class with a much lower multiplier.
  • A recalibration attempt, tried and rejected: we tested refitting the degree-day emergence curve so each bucket's assigned "potential" reproduced that bucket's mean reported severity. That removed the model's mean-level bias almost entirely (predicted mean 1.46 vs. reported 1.49) — but it also collapsed the curve's peak-to-trough range so much that predicted severity never exceeded ~3/5 across the entire dataset, and the model lost the ability to distinguish severe conditions at all (only 23 of 94 truly-"high" reports still predicted high, down from 72 before).
We reverted the recalibration and kept the original curve. An index that's unbiased on average but can't tell severe from mild is worse for this tool's actual purpose than one that runs somewhat high but still ranks severity correctly. Habitat shows a bigger real spread in this data than degree-days do (wet meadow/marsh reports averaged 3.39 vs. open-dry's 1.00), which is the more promising lever for improving severe-case discrimination going forward -- not further curve-fitting against a sample this size.

Known limitations

  • HST reports are self-selected -- posters skew toward reporting notable conditions (both surprisingly good and surprisingly bad) more than "average," which likely inflates both tails of the observed severity distribution relative to true frequency.
  • 381 of 548 observations resolved to a gazetteer coordinate; the rest (vague or compound route descriptions) were correctly left ungeocoded rather than guessed, per design, but that shrinks the usable validation sample further.
  • Only two seasons of data. A proper leave-one-year-out check (fit intuition on 2024, verify against 2025 and vice versa) hasn't been done -- what exists so far is a single in-sample look, appropriate for sanity-checking direction and finding bugs, not for confidently tuning parameters.
  • gridMET weather is ~4km native resolution; grid points closer together than that share identical weather inputs regardless of map spacing.
